About the course

Images and video clips generated by generative models are becoming increasingly difficult to distinguish from real content. In this graduate-level course we will learn the basic principles of generative models; how to design control signals for them and make them efficient; how to use them creatively for a range of visual synthesis tasks; and we will look into some scientific questions about the representations these models learn, and whether those representations are abstract or conceptually grounded.

Selecting your papers. The core set of papers is three or four, selected to be as diverse as possible. Consider it a guideline rather than a final list: either choose all of them, substitute any other papers from the pool for the selected topic, or choose a paper that is listed nowhere else here on the topic. Inform the professor a week in advance of what papers you have chosen, along with one sentence explaining the connection between them.

Lecture readings are background for the term and are not available as presentation papers. A few papers sit in two topics' pools, so claim yours on Canvas when you sign up. Papers marked arXiv have no peer-reviewed venue; a few are canonical enough to present anyway, but say so when you do, and hold them to a higher standard than a reviewed paper.

Everyone will present three times, with at least one of those before Fall Break and at least one after it. Most sessions are two-person teams and only a very few will have third; the instructor assigns the threes when signup closes.

Presentations

You present as a team, and you present a set of papers, at least three of them, not three talks stapled together. A good presentation runs one argument through the set: what the first paper got right, what it left open, what the others did about it.

Format

  • The team has roughly an hour: about 25 minutes each in a pair, about 18 each in a three, plus 5 minutes for questions. Discussion fills the rest of the session.
  • Order the set however you can defend. Chronological is common and not required.
  • Connect back to earlier sessions. The topics are sequenced on purpose.
  • Say what each paper assumes, what it does not show, and where you think it is wrong.
  • Bring two questions you actually want the room to argue about.
  • Prepare sections you can drop. You will run long.

What is graded

The practice and class presentations carry equal weight, at 45% each. The remaining 10% of the grade is based on your slides and a discussion with the TA before practice presentation to demonstrate early preparation.

  • Practice presentation. Monday 11:00–12:00 for that week's Thursday talks, Thursday 11:00–12:00 for the following Tuesday's. This is where the talk actually gets built, so treat it as the real deadline. Before you come to practice, meet Jiahan and walk him through your slides. Book that separately and leave yourself time to act on what he tells you; arriving at practice without the TA review counts as an unprepared practice.
  • Class presentation. Clarity, technical depth, synthesis across the set, how well you run the discussion, and whether you acted on the practice feedback. Three to five classmates grade you anonymously.

Slides are due by the end of the day before your session, as PowerPoint or PDF, submitted on Canvas. Late slides lose credit.

Group project

Work alone or in a team of up to three. The project should do something: reproduce a result and break it, test a claim the literature takes for granted, or build a small thing that works. A negative result reported honestly is worth more than a vague positive one.

Fri Oct 9Proposal, 5%. Half a page. Who is on the team, what you plan to do, and key references with links.
Thu Nov 12Midterm report, 15%. What you have done, how the goals have changed, and evidence that baseline code runs.
Exam dayFinal report, 30%. Four pages, CVPR style template, one submission per team.
Exam daySpotlight video, 50%. Ten minutes. This is the final deliverable everyone sees, so make it watchable: show the result early, and show what failed.

The report needs a cover page with an executive summary and a results figure; an introduction with motivation and background; the approach, with formulas, pseudocode and diagrams; results, with the experimental protocol and example outputs; discussion and conclusions; a statement of individual contributions if you worked in a team; and references. Write it yourself. The four pages must not be AI-written.

Peer grading reports

Each of you grades two sessions and submits four reports in total. They are anonymous to the presenters and they are read, so a report that says only that the talk was good earns nothing.

  • For Tuesday presentations, reports are due by the end of Friday that week.
  • For Thursday presentations, reports are due by the end of the following Sunday.
  • Late reports lose 20% per day, which is 1% of your course grade for each day late.

Use of AI

Use AI to understand a paper. Analyze the derivation with the help of AI, determine the meaning of a term, debate with AI about the questionable assumption is allowed. This is the proper use of the AI tool, although you are supposed to read the paper in its entirety.

Slides are different. Your presentation should be created anew by you. Presentation slides generated by the model cannot be used, nor can you present slides belonging to someone else, including the authors' slides, slides used in another class, or slides presented somewhere online. It is possible and even advisable to borrow one or two slides if they do a job better than you could recreate them in your own words. Use these slides but mention the source in the slide itself.

The four-page final report must be written by you, not by a model. Cite generously all resources that you have used.

Policies

Signup

Sign up for three topics by the end of Thursday, September 3. At least one must fall before Fall Break and at least one after it; do not stack all three in the same half of the term. Missing the deadline costs 10% of your presentation grade. Signing up is a commitment to the instructor and to everyone else in the room, and the schedule has no slack to absorb a team that disappears in the week of its session.

Participation

Come to class and engage, in the room and in the Canvas discussions. A seminar where only the presenting team has read the papers is a bad seminar. Read enough of each core set to ask one real question.

Announcements

Check Canvas for short-notice changes, especially to office hours and practice sessions.

Accessibility

If you need accommodations, contact the university's disability services office and tell the instructor early in the term, so it can be arranged without pressure. If anything about the format here, the presenting, the timing, the practice sessions, is a barrier for you, say so. It can be adjusted.
